          Fentanyl — the latest: what it is, and the way off it

          What it is. Fentanyl is a man-made (synthetic) opioid, about 50 times stronger than heroin. Because it is cheap to make, it is mixed into other drugs — pressed into fake pills made to look like oxycodone (“M30s”), and cut into cocaine, meth, and heroin — often without the person knowing. An amount like a few grains of salt can stop a person’s breathing.

          The latest.

          • Xylazine (“tranq”) — a veterinary sedative now commonly mixed with fentanyl. It is not an opioid, so naloxone cannot reverse xylazine itself — but always give naloxone anyway, because it reverses the fentanyl in the mix. Xylazine also causes severe skin wounds that need medical care.
          • Medetomidine — another powerful sedative turning up in the drug supply in the last couple of years. Same rule: naloxone for the opioid part, 911 for the rest.
          • Fake pills are the biggest danger to young people and first-time users — one pill can kill.
          • Fentanyl test strips are legal in California and can check a supply before use.
          • Starting medicine too soon can backfire: fentanyl builds up in the body, so buprenorphine started too early can throw a person into sudden, severe withdrawal. Providers now often use low-dose starts — one more reason to connect the person to a provider rather than going it alone.

          Overdose — act fast. Won’t wake up; breathing slow, shallow, or stopped; lips or fingertips blue or gray; pinpoint pupils; gurgling sounds. Call 911. Give naloxone. Rescue breathing or CPR if trained. Stay until help arrives — California’s Good Samaritan law protects the caller. If they wake up, they still need a hospital: naloxone wears off in 30–90 minutes and the fentanyl can pull them back under.

          Getting off it — the whole nine yards.

          • Withdrawal starts within about 8–24 hours of last use — anxiety, sweating, muscle aches, nausea, insomnia, intense craving. It is brutal, and the greatest danger is returning to use with a lowered tolerance — that is when overdoses happen.
          • Medicines that help (through a medical provider): buprenorphine (Suboxone), methadone (through a licensed program), and naltrexone (the Vivitrol shot, after detox). They cut craving and withdrawal and lower the risk of dying — the front-line path.
          • Levels of care: withdrawal management (detox), residential programs, outpatient programs, and recovery housing. A person can enter at any level and step up or down as needed.
          • How Centipede helps: peer support. We walk with the person, connect them to San Joaquin County Behavioral Health Services substance-use services — crisis and access line (209) 468-8686, 24/7 — or dial 2-1-1, help with ID, a phone, and transportation, and stay in their corner through the setbacks. We support, encourage, and connect people to care — we do not provide treatment.
          • For families: keep naloxone in the house, never use alone, use test strips, and keep the county crisis line — (209) 468-8686 — where everyone can find it.
