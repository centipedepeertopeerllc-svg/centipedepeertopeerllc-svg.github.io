WHEN YOU FIND SOMEONE — OUTREACH FIELD GUIDE

Centipede Peer to Peer, LLC — for every outreach worker. Built November 28, 2026, at the direction of the Chief Executive Officer, after the night of November 27, 2026 — so that the next time this happens, everything you need is in one place: who to call, and how to handle yourself.

First — check if they are alive

Shout their name. Shake their shoulders. If there is any chance they are alive — and if it could be an overdose (won’t wake up, breathing slow or stopped, lips blue or gray, pinpoint pupils) — call 911, give naloxone (Narcan) if you carry it, start rescue breathing or CPR if you are trained, and stay with them until help arrives. California’s Good Samaritan law protects the person who calls.

If the person has died — the first minutes

  1. Call 911. Say where you are and what you found, plainly and calmly.
  2. Do not touch or move anything. Do not move the person. The scene belongs to the officers and the coroner until they release it.
  3. Keep people back, gently. If it is someone’s home, stay with the resident — it is their home, and their shock.
  4. Write everything down as it happens: the time you found them, the time you called, who arrived and when, officers’ names and badge numbers, what they said, and anything you handed over.
  5. Call the Chief and Jarvis (numbers below), and start an incident report in your dashboard right away — Portal → Incident Reports. Keep adding entries as it unfolds.
  6. Cooperate with the officers. Answer what you know, plainly. If client records or binders are requested, that goes through the Chief — client records carry privacy protections and are released only as the Chief directs.

How to handle yourself

  • Stay calm and slow down. You set the tone for everyone around you.
  • No photos of the person. Ever. No social media posts about it. Ever.
  • Do not guess at a cause of death. That is the coroner’s finding, not ours. If you are asked: “I don’t know — the coroner will determine that.”
  • Guard their dignity and the family’s privacy. Names and details live in the incident record — not in conversation around town.
  • It is normal to be shaken, even if you did not know them well. Tell the Chief or Jarvis. We talk it out the same day — nobody carries one of these alone.
  • Afterwards: wash up, eat something, rest when you can. If it stays with you, the county crisis line — (209) 468-8686 — is there for you too.
Remember: the final report on a death comes as the coroner’s report or the police report. We request both for the person’s file when they are issued — tell Jarvis, and he tracks it until it lands.
