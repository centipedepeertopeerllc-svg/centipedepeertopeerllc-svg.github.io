HIPAA & California Confidentiality — What Every Centipede Staff Member Must Know

Centipede Peer to Peer, LLC — a California for-profit limited liability company · 717 E. Longview Ave., Stockton, CA 95207

Centipede provides non-clinical peer support. This page is staff training material on privacy duties — it is not legal advice. Questions about a specific disclosure go to the CEO.

Every source on this page is an official one — the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), SAMHSA, the California Attorney General, and the official text of California law. Current as of November 18, 2026.

Why This Page Exists

The people we serve trust us with the most private facts of their lives — their health, their history, where they sleep, what they are working through. Keeping that information confidential is not paperwork. It is the job. Three bodies of law set the rules: federal HIPAA, the stricter federal rule for substance use records (42 CFR Part 2), and California's own confidentiality law. Centipede's standing practice is to hold every client's information to the strictest of these standards, at all times, whether or not a given provision reaches us directly — and California law applies to our work in its own right.

What HIPAA Is

HIPAA — the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 — is the federal law that sets national standards for protecting health information. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) writes and enforces its rules through the Office for Civil Rights (OCR).

  • The Privacy Rule is the core of it. It sets the standard for when health information may be used or shared, and when it may not. The basic principle: a person's health information is not used or disclosed except as the Rule permits or requires, or as the person authorizes in writing.
  • PHI — protected health information — is individually identifiable health information in any form: paper, electronic, or spoken. It covers information about a person's past, present, or future physical or mental health or condition, the care they receive, and payment for that care, when it identifies the person — name, address, birth date, and similar identifiers included.
  • The minimum-necessary standard: when information is used or shared for a permitted purpose, only the least amount needed for that purpose goes. Not the whole file. Not the whole story. The minimum necessary.
  • Safeguards: HIPAA requires administrative, physical, and technical safeguards around health information — policies and training, locked storage and controlled access, and passwords and encryption for electronic information (the Security Rule).
  • Breach duties: a breach is an impermissible use or disclosure that compromises the security or privacy of PHI. Under the Breach Notification Rule, breaches of unsecured PHI trigger notification duties — to the affected individuals and to HHS. At Centipede: if you believe client information was seen, sent, or shared wrongly — in any way, however small — tell the CEO the same day. Do not try to fix it quietly.

Substance Use Records — 42 CFR Part 2 (Stricter Than HIPAA)

Federal law gives substance use disorder (SUD) records an extra layer of protection beyond HIPAA: 42 CFR Part 2, administered by SAMHSA. Part 2 protects records of the identity, diagnosis, prognosis, or treatment of a person held by a federally assisted substance use program. The reason for the extra protection is plain: fear of exposure and prosecution keeps people from seeking help.

  • Consent first. Part 2 records generally move only with the person's written consent, under narrow exceptions. A release that is fine for general information may not be enough for Part 2 records.
  • Never against the person. Part 2 records and testimony cannot be used to investigate or prosecute the person they belong to without that person's written consent or a court order meeting Part 2's requirements.
  • The 2024 final rule aligned parts of Part 2 with HIPAA (one consent can cover future treatment, payment, and operations uses; HIPAA-style breach notification applies) while keeping Part 2's extra protections. Compliance with the updated rule is required by February 16, 2026.
  • Why it matters here: Centipede's Substance Abuse Recovery Support work touches this territory. When a client's record involves substance use treatment records from any program, treat those records at the strictest standard on this page — and route any question about sharing them to the CEO before anything moves.

California Law — Our Region

Federal law is the floor, not the ceiling. California has its own confidentiality law, and parts of it are stricter than HIPAA.

  • The Confidentiality of Medical Information Act (CMIA) — California Civil Code §56 et seq. The CMIA generally bars providers of health care, health care service plans, and their contractors from disclosing a person's medical information without that person's written authorization first, subject to specific exceptions, and it carries civil and criminal penalties for violations. If you handle medical information in California, the CMIA is the state rule standing behind it.
  • The Lanterman-Petris-Short (LPS) Act — California Welfare and Institutions Code §5000 et seq. The LPS Act governs involuntary mental-health evaluation and treatment in California, and it makes the records of people served under it confidential — §5328 is the confidentiality provision: information and records obtained under the LPS Act stay confidential except as the Act itself allows.

California law can be stricter than HIPAA, and the stricter rule governs. Where the federal rule and the California rule differ, follow the one that protects the person's information more. When you cannot tell which is stricter, the question goes to the CEO — before anything is shared, not after.

What This Means at Centipede, Day to Day

The disclosure rule — no exceptions, no shortcuts. Client information is disclosed only on one of two grounds:

1. A signed client Release of Information, or
2. A judge-signed court order.

A subpoena alone is NOT a court order. If a subpoena, a request letter, or any demand for client information arrives — from anyone — do not hand anything over. Hold it, notify the CEO, and notify the client. Disclosure moves only on the signed release or the judge-signed order, and only for what it covers.

  • Never write down ID numbers. No Social Security numbers, no ID card numbers, no Veteran ID Card numbers — not in intake, not in notes, not anywhere. Intake holds a copy of the photo ID; the number is never recorded (CPP-POL-2026-043).
  • Minimum necessary, every time. In every conversation, referral, and note, share only what the purpose in front of you actually needs.
  • Client information does not travel by ordinary email and never goes in Google Drive. Client records live in the client's locked binder, which is the official record. Email and encryption duties are set by CPP-POL-2026-025.
  • When in doubt, stop and go to the CEO. No deadline, no requester, and no uniform changes the rule. A disclosure that should not have happened cannot be taken back; a question asked first costs nothing.
  • Suspected breach? Tell the CEO the same day — what happened, what information, whose, and who may have seen it. Same-day, every time.

The Short Version

Client information is private. It moves only on a signed client release or a judge-signed court order. A subpoena alone is not a court order. Share the minimum necessary, never record an ID number, and when in doubt, stop and go to the CEO. HIPAA sets the federal standard, Part 2 protects substance use records more strictly still, and California law — CMIA and the LPS Act — can be stricter than all of it. The stricter rule governs.
