1. The Ellis Act — Government Code §§ 7060–7060.7

The Ellis Act lets a landlord go out of the rental business. It is a limit on government, not an eviction remedy: § 7060(a) bars a public entity from compelling an owner to offer, or keep offering, accommodations for rent. The stated purpose (§ 7060.7) is to supersede Nash v. City of Santa Monica "so as to permit landlords to go out of business." The Act does not supersede relocation assistance law (Gov. Code § 7260 et seq.) — listed first in § 7060.1(d) — nor Part 2 (commencing with Section 43) of Division 1 of the Civil Code, the Civil Code hiring provisions, the unlawful detainer chapter, FEHA, Division 24 (commencing with Section 33000) of the Health and Safety Code, or the Unfair Competition Law (Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200 et seq.). The eviction itself still runs on the ordinary statutes.

  • All units or none. Withdrawal covers all the "accommodations" as § 7060(b)(1) defines them — every unit in a detached structure of four or more, and the parcel groupings the statute specifies. § 7060.7(d)(1): the Act does not permit withdrawing less than all of them. One unit cannot be "Ellised" out from under one tenant.
  • Timelines. Where a city has rent control and has legislated for it: the withdrawal date is 120 days after the owner's notice of intent reaches the public entity (§ 7060.4(b)). A tenant who is 62 or older, or disabled, and has lived in the unit at least one year, may extend their own date to one year — the claim must reach the owner in writing within 60 days (§ 7060.4(b)). The whole property's withdrawal date is the latest termination date among all tenants (§ 7060.4(b)(6)).
  • Restrictions that follow (§ 7060.2). Re-renting within 2 years exposes the owner to actual and exemplary damages; rent on new tenancies is restricted for 5 years to the lawful rent when the notice was filed (§ 7060.2(a)); a displaced tenant who asked in writing within 30 days of displacement holds a right of first refusal a city may extend to 10 years (§ 7060.2(b)(3), (c)).
  • Relocation payment. Withdrawal is a no-fault just cause under Civil Code § 1946.2(b)(2)(B), and § 1946.2(d) requires relocation assistance of one month's rent (paid within 15 calendar days of the notice) or a written waiver of the final month's rent. Failure to strictly comply makes the notice void (§ 1946.2(d)(4)).
  • The tenant's defense. In an unlawful detainer, the tenant may defend on the ground that the owner did not comply with the Ellis Act chapter or the local law implementing it — § 7060.6.

2. Housing discrimination — FEHA and the Unruh Act

California's Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA), Government Code § 12955, makes it unlawful to discriminate in the sale, rental, or provision of housing — including refusing a dwelling, setting discriminatory terms, or publishing a discriminatory notice or advertisement (§ 12955(a), (c), (k)). The protected characteristics under § 12955(a) are:

race · color · religion · sex · gender · gender identity · gender expression · sexual orientation · marital status · national origin · ancestry · familial status · source of income · disability · veteran or military status · genetic information

  • Source of income counts. Under § 12955(p), "source of income" includes housing subsidies — including Section 8 Housing Choice Vouchers (42 U.S.C. § 1437f) and HUD-VASH vouchers. Refusing a tenant because they pay with a voucher is discrimination.
  • Disability. Disability discrimination in housing is unlawful under § 12955, and FEHA carries the duty of reasonable accommodation for people with disabilities. ("Disabled" in the Ellis Act itself is defined through § 12955.3 and § 12926.)
  • Unruh Civil Rights Act — Civil Code § 51. Every person is entitled to full and equal accommodations and services in all business establishments, regardless of sex, race, color, religion, ancestry, national origin, disability, medical condition, genetic information, marital status, sexual orientation, citizenship, primary language, or immigration status.

3. More tenant protections our work runs into

  • Just cause — Civil Code § 1946.2. After 12 months of tenancy, a tenancy can be ended only for a listed just cause (§ 1946.2(a), (b)). A notice that does not comply is void (§ 1946.2(g)), and a material violation carries actual damages, up to treble damages, and punitive damages (§ 1946.2(h)(1)).
  • Notice periods — Civil Code § 1946.1. 60 days to end a periodic tenancy; 30 days if the tenant has been there less than a year (§ 1946.1(b), (c)).
  • Retaliation — Civil Code § 1942.5. A landlord may not recover possession, raise rent, or cut services within 180 days of a tenant's protected activity — complaining about habitability, exercising legal rights, or organizing with other tenants (§ 1942.5(a), (d)).
  • Habitability — Civil Code § 1941.1. This section lists the conditions that make a dwelling untenantable under California law. Flag: the official category list is held for re-verification and is set out in the packet when verified — ask Jarvis or the CEO before relying on specifics.
  • Rent cap — Civil Code § 1947.12. The Tenant Protection Act's statewide cap on annual rent increases. Flag: the exact formula is carried in the packet as UNVERIFIED pending re-verification against the official text.
