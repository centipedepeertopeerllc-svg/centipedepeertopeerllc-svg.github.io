HOW TO USE THIS MANUAL

This manual is the one place that says how peer support is done at Centipede Peer to Peer, LLC. Every peer supporter, supervisor, and volunteer who works with the people we serve reads it, works by it, and is trained from it.

The standards in this manual stand on two legs:

1. SAMHSA — the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration of the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services. SAMHSA is the national authority on peer support. Centipede follows SAMHSA's framework: its Core Competencies for Peer Workers, its recovery model, its practice guidelines for peer supporters, and its guidance on trauma-informed care. Where this manual states a standard of practice, the standard tracks SAMHSA unless a Centipede policy says more.

2. Centipede's own policies — the written policies of this company, cited by number where they apply (for example, CPP-POL-2026-043, the Intake Before Membership Policy). Company policy is fitted inside the SAMHSA framework, never against it.

One rule runs through every page: Centipede provides non-clinical peer support only. Nothing in this manual makes any employee a clinician, and nothing in it authorizes clinical work of any kind.

CHAPTER 1 — WHO WE ARE

1.1 The company. Centipede Peer to Peer, LLC is a California for-profit limited liability company based in Stockton, California. We say that plainly, everywhere: we are a for-profit company. We are not a nonprofit, not a charity, and not a government agency, and no company material may state or imply otherwise.

1.2 A family-run company. Centipede is a family-run company, under the company's Family Run Company Standard (adopted November 13, 2026). Ownership and control stay in the family; final decisions rest with the Chief Executive Officer. The founder's values — honesty, keeping your word, and treating people right — are the culture. The family name stands behind the work, which is why the record is kept clean and every document is done properly. People are not numbers: the people we serve are treated the way the family would want its own treated. Nobody walks alone.

1.3 A peer-run company. Centipede's work is done by people with lived experience — people who have themselves lived through homelessness, mental health conditions, substance use, the justice system, or the work of supporting a family member through them, and who have built a life in recovery. Lived experience is not a biographical detail here; it is the qualification the whole model stands on. As SAMHSA puts it, peer support is "offering and receiving help, based on shared understanding, respect and mutual empowerment between people in similar situations."

1.4 What recovery means. Centipede uses SAMHSA's working definition of recovery: "Recovery is a process of change through which individuals improve their health and wellness, live self-directed lives, and strive to reach their full potential." Recovery is a process, not an end state, and there are many pathways to it. SAMHSA names four dimensions that support a life in recovery, and Centipede's peer supporters work in all four: Health, Home, Purpose, and Community.

1.5 SAMHSA's guiding principles of recovery. SAMHSA's Working Definition of Recovery (PEP12-RECDEF) sets out ten guiding principles, and they are Centipede's principles too: recovery emerges from hope; recovery is person-driven; recovery occurs via many pathways; recovery is holistic; recovery is supported by peers and allies; recovery is supported through relationships and social networks; recovery is culturally based and influenced; recovery is supported by addressing trauma; recovery involves individual, family, and community strengths and responsibility; and recovery is based on respect.

CHAPTER 2 — WHAT PEER SUPPORT IS AND IS NOT

2.1 What peer support is. A Centipede peer supporter does the following, and only the following, with the people we serve:

  • Support — stand beside the person, in person, over time.
  • Encourage — hold out hope and recognize effort and progress.
  • Coach — help the person practice skills and strategies they choose for their own goals.
  • Mentor — model a life in recovery by living one.
  • Navigate — walk with the person through systems, paperwork, and appointments so they are not facing them alone.
  • Connect to resources — know the community's resources and link the person to the ones they choose.
  • Share lived experience — tell one's own recovery story, at the right time and for the person's benefit (Chapter 4).
  • Facilitate groups — lead peer groups where peers support each other.
  • Advocate — speak with and for the person, at their direction, so their rights and choices are respected.

2.2 What peer support is not. Centipede is a non-clinical company. No Centipede employee, in any program, at any time:

  • Diagnoses any person, or states or implies a diagnosis.
  • Provides treatment, therapy, or clinical counseling.
  • Performs a clinical assessment of any kind.
  • Gives medical, psychiatric, or legal advice.
  • Adjusts, recommends, or comments on medication as a clinical matter. (Helping a person keep their own appointments and their own routine, at their request, is peer support. Advising them about their medicine is not.)
  • Uses the words "treatment," "therapy," "counseling" (in the clinical sense), "diagnose," or "clinical assessment" to describe Centipede's services, or calls any staff member a therapist, counselor, or clinician.

2.3 Why the line matters. The line is not red tape. It protects the people we serve, who deserve to know exactly what kind of help they are getting. It protects the peer supporter, who is never asked to carry clinical responsibility they are not trained or licensed to carry. And it protects the company, whose word — given plainly and kept — is its standing in this community. When a need is clinical, the peer supporter's job is to connect the person to a licensed provider and to tell the CEO (Chapters 5 and 10).

2.4 No promises. Peer supporters describe the services Centipede offers. They never promise results — no promised housing, no promised benefits, no promised outcomes of any kind. We say what we will do, and we do what we said. That is the whole of it.

CHAPTER 3 — THE SAMHSA CORE COMPETENCIES, AS CENTIPEDE PRACTICES THEM

SAMHSA's Core Competencies for Peer Workers in Behavioral Health Services (2015, developed through SAMHSA's Bringing Recovery Supports to Scale Technical Assistance Center Strategy — BRSS TACS) is the backbone of peer practice at Centipede. The competencies rest on five foundational principles, which every Centipede peer supporter is expected to work by:

  • Recovery-oriented: we hold out hope, partner with people to envision and achieve a meaningful and purposeful life, build on strengths, and recognize that there are multiple pathways to recovery.
  • Person-centered: services are always directed by the person receiving them, and are shaped to that person's own hopes, goals, and preferences.
  • Voluntary: we are partners and consultants, never directors. Participation in peer support is always the person's choice.
  • Relationship-focused: the relationship is the foundation. It is respectful, trusting, empathetic, collaborative, and mutual.
  • Trauma-informed: we work from a strengths-based framework that emphasizes physical, psychological, and emotional safety (Chapter 5).

The twelve competency categories follow. Each is stated the way it is practiced at Centipede.

Category I — Engages peers in collaborative and caring relationships. At Centipede: we initiate contact and keep it; we listen with careful attention to both the content and the emotion being communicated; we reach out across the whole continuum of a person's recovery, not only when things go well; we show genuine acceptance and respect; and we work to understand each person's experiences and feelings as they describe them.

Category II — Provides support. At Centipede: we validate people's experiences and feelings; we encourage people to explore and take up roles in their community; we convey hope about the person's own recovery; we celebrate their efforts and accomplishments; and we give concrete, practical help toward the tasks and goals the person has chosen.

Category III — Shares lived experiences of recovery. At Centipede: we relate our own recovery stories — and, with permission, the stories of others — to inspire hope; we talk honestly about our own ongoing efforts toward health, wellness, and recovery; we learn to recognize when to share and when to listen; and we describe the recovery practices that work for us while helping each person discover the practices that work for them (Chapter 4).

Category IV — Personalizes peer support. At Centipede: we know our own values and culture and how they can bias our judgment; we appreciate and respect the cultural and spiritual beliefs and practices of the people we serve and their families; we recognize that every person's recovery process is unique; and we tailor support to each person's preferences and needs.

Category V — Supports recovery planning. At Centipede: we help people set goals and dream of future possibilities; we propose strategies for accomplishing the tasks and goals the person chooses; we support people in using decision-making strategies when choosing services and supports; we help people take their place as members of their own support teams; and we research and identify credible information and options. The person's plan is the person's own — recorded in the Plan section of the DAP note, with an owner and a date for every step (Chapter 7).

Category VI — Links to resources, services, and supports. At Centipede: we develop and maintain current knowledge of community resources and services — in Stockton and San Joaquin County first; we assist people to investigate, select, and use the resources they need and want; we help people find and use health services and supports; and we accompany people to community activities and appointments when they ask us to.

Category VII — Provides information about skills related to health, wellness, and recovery. At Centipede: we share information about health, wellness, recovery, and recovery supports; we learn alongside the people we serve; we coach people on how to reach services and navigate systems of care; we coach the skills and strategies a person wants to build; we share recovery information with family members and other supporters, with the person's permission; and we use approaches that match each person's preferences and needs. Information is shared as information — never as clinical instruction or advice.

Category VIII — Helps peers to manage crises. At Centipede: we recognize signs of distress and threats to safety; we provide reassurance to people in distress; we work to create safe spaces when we meet; we take action in a crisis using knowledge of local resources and of the person's own stated preferences; and we help people prepare their own crisis-prevention tools in advance, in calmer times. Chapter 10 states the company's crisis procedure.

Category IX — Values communication. At Centipede: we use respectful, person-centered, recovery-oriented language, in speech and in writing, with the people we serve, their families, community members, and each other; we use active listening; we clarify our understanding when we are in doubt; we state our point of view honestly with colleagues; we document as company policy requires; and we follow the law and company rules on confidentiality (Chapter 8).

Category X — Supports collaboration and teamwork. At Centipede: we work together as colleagues to strengthen the support each person receives; we engage assertively with service providers — in mental health, substance use, and physical health services — to meet the needs of the people we serve; we coordinate with the person's other providers, and with family members and natural supports, only with the person's permission and within the confidentiality rules; we partner with community members and organizations; and we work to resolve conflicts in relationships respectfully.

Category XI — Promotes leadership and advocacy. At Centipede: we use our knowledge of people's rights and of the law — including the ADA and HIPAA — to help make sure rights are respected; we advocate for the needs and desires of the people we serve in team meetings, with community services, in living situations, and with family, always at the person's direction; we use knowledge of legal resources and advocacy organizations to build advocacy plans; we take part in efforts to end prejudice and discrimination against people with behavioral health conditions and their families; we teach colleagues about recovery and peer support; we take an active part in improving this company; and we keep a good name in the peer and professional community.

Category XII — Promotes growth and development. At Centipede: we recognize the limits of our own knowledge and ask for help when we need it; we use supervision well — monitoring ourselves and our relationships, preparing for supervision meetings, and solving problems with our supervisor; we reflect on our own motivations, judgments, and feelings that the work stirs up, recognize our own signs of distress, and know when to seek support; and we look for opportunities to grow our knowledge and skill (Chapter 12).

CHAPTER 4 — THE PEER SUPPORT RELATIONSHIP

4.1 The relationship is the work. Everything a peer supporter does rests on a relationship of shared understanding, respect, and mutual empowerment between people in similar situations. The National Practice Guidelines for Peer Supporters — developed by the International Association of Peer Supporters (iNAPS, 2013), reviewed by a peer leadership panel at SAMHSA, and endorsed by peer supporters across the United States — name twelve core values of peer support. They are Centipede's values, word for word:

1. Peer support is voluntary. 2. Peer supporters are hopeful. 3. Peer supporters are open minded. 4. Peer supporters are empathetic. 5. Peer supporters are respectful. 6. Peer supporters facilitate change. 7. Peer supporters are honest and direct. 8. Peer support is mutual and reciprocal. 9. Peer support is equally shared power. 10. Peer support is strengths-focused. 11. Peer support is transparent. 12. Peer support is person-driven.

4.2 Engagement. Engagement starts with showing up — reliably, at the agreed time and place, doing what was said. Trust at Centipede is built the family way: our word, kept, over time. The peer supporter listens first and lets the person finish. We do not interrupt. We do not talk over. We respond to what the person actually said, not to what we expected them to say.

4.3 Listening. Listening is a skill, not a pause between speeches. Listen with careful attention to the content and the emotion. Repeat back what you heard when it matters, and check that you heard it right. Silence is allowed. A person deciding what they think is not a gap for the peer supporter to fill.

4.4 Boundaries. The peer relationship is a working relationship, warm and bounded at the same time:

  • Keep the agreed schedule and the agreed meeting places. Changes go through the supervisor or the CEO, not side arrangements.
  • Company contact channels only: the company phone, the company email, the Portal. No personal social-media friending or private messaging with the people we serve.
  • The peer supporter does not lend or give personal money, does not hold a person's money, cards, keys, medication, or property, and does not make purchases for a person outside the company's purchase-order process.
  • The peer supporter does not transport a person in a personal vehicle; transport follows company policy and assigned vehicles only.
  • If a relationship starts to feel like friendship, family, or romance rather than peer support, that is a supervision matter: raise it with the supervisor or the CEO the same day. Wanting to help more than the role allows is a common and honest feeling; acting on it outside the role is where harm starts.

4.5 Sharing lived experience. Self-disclosure is the peer supporter's distinctive tool, and like any tool it has a right use:

  • Share for the person's benefit, never for your own relief. The test before speaking: "Will this help them, in their situation, right now?"
  • Share briefly and return the focus. Your story opens a door; their story is the room.
  • Share what recovery looks like in practice — what you did, what helped, what you still do — not graphic detail of the worst times, which can burden or retraumatize the listener (Chapter 5).
  • Never present your pathway as the pathway. There are many pathways to recovery; theirs may not look like yours.
  • Share another person's story only with that person's permission, and never with anything that identifies them.

4.6 Respect, culture, and language. Use the name and words the person uses for themselves. Use person-first, recovery-oriented language: a person is not their condition, their charge, or their situation. Respect cultural and spiritual beliefs and practices, including the ones you do not share. If you do not understand something about a person's culture, ask respectfully or ask your supervisor — do not guess and do not judge.

4.7 Endings and transitions. People leave services, move, or finish their goals. Endings are handled openly: the person knows in advance, the record is completed, referrals are offered, and the door is left open in words and in fact. No one is dropped without a word. Nobody walks alone applies at the end of the work too.

CHAPTER 5 — TRAUMA-INFORMED PEER SUPPORT

5.1 Why this chapter stands on its own. Most of the people Centipede serves have lived through trauma — violence, loss, homelessness itself, institutional harm, combat, abuse, neglect, or the long grind of being unheard by the systems meant to help. SAMHSA is direct about this: trauma is widespread, its effects are lasting, and services that ignore it can do harm. A peer supporter who does not understand trauma will, sooner or later, hurt someone while trying to help. This chapter is the whole nine yards of how Centipede works with trauma — as peers.

5.2 What trauma is. SAMHSA's Concept of Trauma and Guidance for a Trauma-Informed Approach (2014) defines individual trauma as resulting from "an event, series of events, or set of circumstances that is experienced by an individual as physically or emotionally harmful or life threatening and that has lasting adverse effects on the individual's functioning and mental, physical, social, emotional, or spiritual well-being." Three things follow for peer work: trauma is defined by the person's experience, not by anyone else's judgment of the event; its effects can appear in any part of a person's life; and a person's reactions that look difficult — guardedness, anger, shutting down, leaving — are often adaptations that once kept them safe.

5.3 SAMHSA's six principles of a trauma-informed approach, as Centipede practices them:

1. Safety. The person feels physically and psychologically safe with us. Meet where the person feels safest. Keep your voice and your movements calm and predictable. Never corner, crowd, or surprise. Ask before sitting close, before taking notes, before anything that could feel like control. 2. Trustworthiness and transparency. We do what we said we would do, and we say plainly what we can and cannot do, what we write down, who sees it, and what happens next. No hidden steps. If something changes, the person hears it from us, first and directly. 3. Peer support. This principle is SAMHSA naming our own model: people with lived experience of trauma and recovery are the credible messengers of hope. Our stories, shared well (Chapter 4), are how trust is built with people who have reason not to trust. 4. Collaboration and mutuality. We work WITH the person, not on them. Power differences are named and leveled wherever they can be: the person chooses goals, chooses pace, and can say no — and a no is respected the first time. 5. Empowerment, voice and choice. We build on strengths. The person's voice is the lead voice in their own life. We offer choices — real ones — including the choice of how much to tell us, and when. 6. Cultural, historical, and gender issues. We recognize that trauma is shaped by culture, history, and gender — including the trauma communities carry across generations and the harm done by systems themselves. We do not stereotype; we learn from the person in front of us, and we respect the healing practices of their own culture and faith.

5.4 Avoiding retraumatization. Retraumatization happens when a service repeats the shape of the original harm: powerlessness, being trapped, being disbelieved, being punished for a reaction. Peer supporters guard against it by practice:

  • Never demand the trauma story. A person never has to tell us what happened to them to receive peer support. If they choose to tell, listen; do not probe for detail.
  • Give warning and choice before difficult topics, paperwork, or visits from other agencies. Surprises retraumatize.
  • Watch for signs of distress — changed breathing, agitation, going quiet or blank, wanting to leave — and slow down, ground in the present (where we are, what day it is, what happens next), and offer to stop. Stopping is always allowed.
  • Do not use threats, ultimatums, or withdrawal of support to force compliance. Support at Centipede is never a lever.
  • Keep the environment steady: same meeting rhythm, same explanations, the same person where we can. Predictability is safety.

5.5 The hard boundary: trauma work that is NOT peer work. Talking through trauma as treatment — trauma therapy, trauma counseling, exposure work, clinical processing of traumatic events — is clinical work. It belongs to licensed clinicians. Centipede does not provide it, no employee is authorized to attempt it, and no one may describe Centipede as providing it. A peer supporter who finds that a person's need is for trauma treatment does three things: (1) stays with the person in the peer role — presence, calm, and support are still ours to give; (2) offers connection to a licensed provider, with the person's agreement, as a warm referral (Chapter 10); and (3) reports the need to the CEO the same day, so the referral is tracked and followed up. Recognizing the edge of our scope and acting on it is not a failure of peer support. It is peer support, done right.

5.6 The peer supporter's own trauma. This work touches our own histories. Every Centipede peer supporter has the standing right — and the standing instruction — to name it when the work stirs up their own trauma, to step back from a situation that is becoming unsafe for them, and to take it to supervision without penalty and without shame (Chapter 12). Taking care of the peer supporter is part of taking care of the people we serve.

CHAPTER 6 — INTAKE: CPP-POL-2026-043

6.1 The rule. Intake comes first. No person becomes a member of Centipede Peer to Peer, LLC, or of any company program, until an intake for that person has been completed and approved. Enrollment in any program follows an approved intake; there is no enrollment without one. (CPP-POL-2026-043, Intake Before Membership Policy.)

6.2 Where intake lives. Intakes are opened and worked in the Client Dashboard inside the Company Portal. Each intake carries its own number (INT-####). The client's locked binder is the official record; the approved intake is carried into the binder when the member's binder is opened.

6.3 What intake collects. The intake asks every question that applies: who the person is and how they prefer to be addressed; how to reach them safely; their living situation; an emergency contact, with permission; how they found us; what they need help with and the programs they are asking about; income and benefits, in general terms; documents held; health and support needs in peer terms only; safety and urgent needs; justice information when it applies; veteran information when it applies; and the person's own goals and preferences, including anything they do not want done. A blank is an honest answer, never a guess. Intake is a conversation, not an interrogation — Chapter 5 applies: the person chooses how much to tell, and nothing in intake requires a person to recount traumatic events.

6.4 The three documents required to approve an intake: (a) Government photo ID — a copy is held in the binder; the ID number is never recorded. (b) Signed Release of Information (ROI) / consent. (c) Signed Intake & Participation Agreement. An intake may be approved only when all three are in. Other documents apply as they stand — an insurance card copy, or the person is routed to the Clients Without Insurance list; proof of living situation where it exists, with a signed self-declaration standing when no document exists; Justice Support court paperwork and attorney-of-record information; Veterans DD-214 or VA documentation of service. Each document is marked in the dashboard as it stands — received, self-declared, does not apply, or still to come — and followed up.

6.5 Approval and what follows. On approval the person becomes a member: the dashboard assigns the next client code (CP-####) and client number, and the person appears in the Clients list for encounters, referrals, enrollments, and documents. Approval is recorded with its date in the intake register. A person whose intake is not approved is still offered referrals and resource navigation. Intake decides membership — it never decides whether someone is treated with respect.

6.6 What is never collected. No intake field records an identification number of any kind: no Social Security number, no ID card number, no Veteran ID Card number. If a number appears on a document a person shows us, we hold the copy as policy directs and we do not write the number down anywhere — not in the dashboard, not in a note, not on paper.

CHAPTER 7 — DOCUMENTATION: THE DAP STANDARD

7.1 The record is the work's witness. If it is not written down, it did not happen — and if it is written down wrong, worse than not happening, it misleads everyone who relies on it later. Every contact with a person we serve is documented the same day it happens. Same day means the same calendar day, before the peer supporter's shift ends. There are no exceptions and no catching up from memory days later.

7.2 The one structure: DAP. Every client note at Centipede uses the mandatory DAP skeleton (Form DAP-SKEL-01, approved final November 9, 2026). No client note is drafted, approved, or filed in any other structure. DAP stands for Data, Assessment, Plan.

  • D — Data (what happened). Record only what was directly observed and what the client said. Quote the client's own words exactly, in quotation marks. Do not rewrite the client's words into clinical or professional terms. Do not infer absent facts: if it was not said or seen, it does not go in this section; mark gaps plainly as "Not observed" or "Not stated." No opinions and no conclusions in this section.
  • A — Assessment (peer observation and actions taken). This is a PEER observation: what the peer supporter observed, and the actions the peer supporter took — support offered, resources provided, referrals made, advocacy performed, strengths observed. This is NOT a clinical assessment. Never state or imply a diagnosis anywhere in this section.
  • P — Plan (next steps and follow-up). Record the agreed next steps. Every item carries an owner and a date. Items carried forward from the prior note's Plan are marked CARRIED FORWARD and either closed here or listed again — never silently dropped. The Plan becomes the opening check of the next session.

7.3 The restricted diagnosis block. The DAP skeleton carries one restricted block for provider-documented diagnoses (DSM-5 / ICD-10). A code is entered in that block only where a licensed provider has already documented that diagnosis, with the provider's name and the source document recorded next to the code. If no provider documentation is on file, the block is left blank or marked "None recorded." A client's own report of a diagnosis is recorded in the Data section, in the client's own words — never entered as a code. Centipede does not diagnose; this block exists so that paperwork from licensed providers can be filed accurately, nothing more.

7.4 Language in notes. Notes use respectful, person-centered, recovery-oriented language (Competency IX). Write what you would be content for the client, the CEO, and — on a lawful order — a court to read. Because someday one of them will.

7.5 Where the record lives. The client's locked binder is the official record. Dashboards in the Company Portal are the working surface; Jarvis carries dashboard material into the binders, and the binders govern whenever the two differ (CPP-POL-2026-044). Client case material is never filed in a program binder, never sent by email, and never placed in Google Drive. Program documents are filed in the program's own binder under the same document number the dashboard assigned, the same day.

CHAPTER 8 — CONFIDENTIALITY

8.1 The standing rule. Client information at Centipede is held at a HIPAA level of confidentiality, permanently. What a person tells us, what we observe, and what their paperwork shows are theirs. They are not conversation, not content, and not ours to share.

8.2 Disclosure — the only two doors. Client information is disclosed outside the company only on: (1) a signed client Release of Information (ROI), covering only what the release covers, to only whom it names; or (2) a judge-signed court order, covering only what the order covers. A subpoena alone is NOT a court order. On receiving a subpoena or any other request for client information — from any agency, any attorney, any office, however official it looks — the peer supporter does not disclose anything, does not confirm or deny that the person is a client, and takes the request to the CEO the same day. The CEO holds the matter from there.

8.3 Inside the company. Client information is shared inside Centipede only with the team members working with that person, and only what they need to do the work. Client names and details never appear on public calendars or public material; public surfaces carry program-level information only.

8.4 What never gets recorded. Social Security numbers and full identification numbers are never recorded anywhere in company systems — not in intake, not in notes, not on purchase orders, not spoken into any recording (Chapter 6.6). A Veteran ID Card is recorded as held or not held — never its number.

8.5 Conversations in the field. Do not discuss a person's situation where others can hear. Do not confirm to neighbors, family, other clients, or anyone else that a person is served by Centipede, except with the person's permission or as their work requires (for example, an introduction the person has agreed to). When in doubt, say less and ask the CEO.

8.6 Reporting duties that stand above confidentiality. Two duties outrank confidentiality, and every peer supporter knows them: suspected abuse or neglect of a child (San Joaquin County CPS 24-hour hotline: (209) 468-1333; if a child is in immediate danger, call 911 first), and an emergency in which someone may be seriously harmed (call 911, Chapter 10). Where the law requires a report, make the report and tell the CEO the same day.

CHAPTER 9 — FIELD WORK

9.1 Where the work happens. Centipede's field work is done in homes, at the office, in encampments and other unsheltered locations, at community sites, and alongside people at their appointments. Home and office visits are the company's standing service setting.

9.2 Before you go. Know the person's Plan from the last DAP note and open your visit with it. Know where you are going, tell the office or your supervisor where you will be and when you expect to be back, and carry the company phone. Visits are scheduled and known — nobody freelances a visit.

9.3 In the home and in the field. You are a guest. Announce yourself, wait to be invited in, and respect the household's rules. Keep the visit to its purpose and its agreed length. In encampments and unsheltered locations, approach openly, identify yourself and the company, and accept a refusal of contact with the same respect as an acceptance — outreach is an offer, renewed patiently, never pressure.

9.4 Safety. Your safety is a company rule, not a personal preference:

  • If a scene is not safe, leave and call 911. You cannot help anyone if you are hurt.
  • Do not enter a space where weapons are being handled or where violence is underway. Withdraw and call 911.
  • Work in pairs where the CEO or supervisor directs it, and follow check-in times exactly.
  • Trust your own read of a situation. Leaving early is always an acceptable decision; report it the same day, without apology.

9.5 Working alongside the CEO. The CEO works in the field personally, including daily peer support visits. When the CEO is present, the CEO leads; the peer supporter supports, documents, and follows up. Field assignments, changes of assignment, and anything unusual in the field are reported to the CEO the same day.

9.6 After the visit. Document the same day (Chapter 7). Complete the Plan items you own. Flag anything the next visit must open with. Field work ends at the record, not at the car door.

CHAPTER 10 — CRISIS AND EMERGENCIES

10.1 The first rule. In an emergency — a medical emergency, a person in immediate danger, violence underway or about to happen — call 911. That rule outranks every other procedure in this manual.

10.2 What a peer supporter does in a crisis (SAMHSA Competency VIII; the company's Mental Health Crisis Response Guide):

  • Stay calm and stay present. One voice, low and steady. Keep the person talking.
  • Make sure you are safe first. If the scene is not safe, leave and call 911.
  • Stay with the person if it is safe to do so. Do not leave someone in crisis alone when you can safely remain, and do not hang up first on a crisis call.
  • Listen without arguing. Do not debate what the person is experiencing. Acknowledge distress plainly: "You don't have to go through this alone."
  • If the person may harm themselves, ask directly and take every answer seriously. Direct questions do not plant ideas; they open doors.
  • Use the right line for the need: 911 for emergencies and immediate danger; 988 (call or text, 24 hours a day) for the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline; the county mobile crisis and CPS lines as the Crisis Response Guide directs.
  • A warm handoff means the person is connected — a live voice, a named responder, a confirmed next step — before you step back. Handing someone a phone number and leaving is not a warm handoff.

10.3 After a crisis. Document the same day, factually, in the DAP structure: what was observed and said (Data), what you did (Assessment), and what follows (Plan). Report the crisis to the CEO the same day, whatever the hour the situation allows. A crisis the company does not hear about is a crisis the company cannot follow up.

10.4 The peer scope in crisis. Crisis work by a peer supporter is presence, de-escalation, connection, and follow-up. It is not clinical risk assessment, not involuntary-hold evaluation, and not crisis therapy. Those belong to the responders and clinicians we connect people to. Knowing the edge of the role in the worst moment is part of the role.

CHAPTER 11 — ETHICS AND CONDUCT

11.1 The standard. Centipede's ethics are the peer values of Chapter 4, practiced when nobody is watching. SAMHSA's National Model Standards for Peer Support Certification call for codes of ethics covering the defined role and its scope, personal and professional boundaries, prevention of conflicts of interest, confidentiality, and mandated reporting. This chapter is Centipede's code, in those terms.

11.2 Conflicts of interest. A peer supporter does not use a person's trust for private advantage — no business deals with the people we serve, no borrowing, no hiring a client privately, no steering a person toward any service for anyone's benefit but their own. If a situation could look like a conflict, disclose it to the CEO before it ripens, not after.

11.3 Gifts. Small tokens of gratitude are a human reality in this work; cash and valuables are not acceptable, ever. When a gift is offered: thank the person warmly, decline cash and anything of real value plainly and kindly, and report any gift you could not gracefully refuse to your supervisor the same day. No gift changes anyone's service. [Company note: Centipede has no separate written gifts policy as of this draft; this section states the standing practice for CEO confirmation on adoption.]

11.4 Dual relationships. Many Centipede staff live in the community we serve and will meet the people we serve at the store, at meetings, and at church. Outside contact is handled openly: greet the person as they wish to be greeted (some prefer not to be identified as a client — follow their lead), keep it brief and social, and never discuss their case outside the work. A pre-existing personal relationship with a person who becomes a client is disclosed to the CEO so assignments can be set fairly.

11.5 Social media and public speech. Employees do not post about clients — no names, no stories, no photographs, no "anonymous" accounts that anyone could trace. Employees do not speak for the company publicly unless the CEO has given them that role. What an employee posts personally never carries client information and never presents Centipede's programs as other than what they are, with their true status labels (Chapter 13). The CEO reviews public posts that name the company before they publish.

11.6 Honesty in the record and in the room. No note is ever backdated, padded, or written for a contact that did not happen. No credential is claimed that was not earned (Chapter 12). A mistake in the record is corrected by a dated addition, never by quietly rewriting history.

11.7 Conduct with colleagues. We do not interrupt each other or the people we serve; we let speakers finish; we handle disagreement inside the company, on the record, and settle it. The way we treat each other is the culture the people we serve walk into.

CHAPTER 12 — SUPERVISION, TRAINING, AND CERTIFICATION

12.1 Supervision. Peer supporters at Centipede work under supervision — by the CEO directly, or by a supervisor the CEO names. Supervision is not inspection; it is the structure that keeps the work honest and the worker supported. A peer supporter:

  • Brings their open Plans, difficult moments, and boundary questions to supervision — prepared, not ambushed.
  • Raises scope questions before acting, whenever time allows.
  • Reports crises, safety events, disclosure requests, and anything unusual the same day (Chapters 8, 9, 10).
  • Uses supervision for their own growth (Competency XII): reflecting on motivations, judgments, and feelings the work stirs up, and naming their own signs of distress early.

12.2 Training. New peer supporters are trained from this manual, the DAP skeleton, and the company's crisis response guide before carrying a caseload, and are accompanied in the field until the CEO or supervisor judges them ready to work independently. Training continues on the job: supervision, team review of practice against the SAMHSA competencies, and community training opportunities as the CEO assigns them. Company training records are kept in the employee's HR file.

12.3 Certification — the honest position. California's route to certification as a peer support specialist is the state program administered through the California Mental Health Services Authority (CalMHSA): a state-approved training and examination leading to certification as a Medi-Cal Peer Support Specialist. That certification belongs to the state process. Centipede does not operate a certification program and does not issue state certification; no company document may state or imply that it does. Where the company supports an employee toward certification, it supports them toward the CalMHSA route — study time, training access, and encouragement — and the credential, when earned, is theirs. Company-issued documents record training completed; they are not certificates of state certification.

12.4 The peer supporter's own recovery. Employment at Centipede asks lived experience, and it protects it. A peer supporter's own recovery is their own; the company does not require disclosure of any diagnosis or history beyond what the person chooses to share for the work. A hard season in a peer supporter's own life is a supervision conversation, met with support and workload honesty — the same peer values we offer the people we serve apply to each other (Chapter 5.6).
